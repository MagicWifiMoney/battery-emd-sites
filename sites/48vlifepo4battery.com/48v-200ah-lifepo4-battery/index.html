Why this table has one row

It is not a tracking gap — it is the market. Makers sell '48V 200Ah' three ways, and only one of them is a standalone product:

  • A true single-case unit — the wall-mounted listing in the table below is the only one in the maker stores we track today.
  • A variant selector: Renogy's 51.2V battery is one product page covering both 100Ah and 200Ah configurations (a single URL, four feed variants). This tracker lists each maker URL once, in its 100Ah configuration — so that 200Ah option lives on our 100Ah table, not here.
  • A multi-unit kit: at least one '200Ah-class' offer is four 50Ah units bundled as a 9.6kWh bank — a bank wearing a single battery's label.

The demand the phrase represents is real (it is searched every month); the standalone product mostly is not. Buyers who go looking for one case almost always end up with two 100Ah modules instead — the rest of this page is about making that call with live numbers.

The 9.6kWh vs 10.24kWh trap

Both numbers come from the same multiplication with different voltages. Lead-acid-era math uses the nominal system voltage: 48V × 200Ah = 9,600Wh. LiFePO4 math uses the pack's real nominal voltage: 51.2V × 200Ah = 10,240Wh. Makers use both — the one direct listing below is a true 10,240Wh unit, while the kit configuration mentioned above is rated 9,600Wh for the same '200Ah' label.

The trap costs real money: a 9,600Wh unit has to be about 6% cheaper than a 10,240Wh unit just to tie on value. Sticker prices hide that; $/Wh cannot. Sort the table by $/Wh (it already is) and the honest ranking falls out — this is the entire reason this site ranks by watt-hours instead of amp-hours.

What the class lacks is choice. The big value brands pour their volume into 100Ah modules instead, so a shopper cross-shopping '200Ah in one case' against '200Ah as two modules' will find a one-row table here and a long one on the 100Ah page.

That thinness is itself the buying signal: if the exact 200Ah unit you want is out of stock or priced at a premium, the two-module build is not a compromise — it is the mainstream product, with the deeper discounting. Our 10kWh page prices both paths side by side from the full catalog.

Who should skip the 200Ah class

  • Incremental builders: the first step is the whole ~10kWh. Modules let you start at 5.12kWh and grow.
  • $/Wh-first buyers: the module market is deeper and usually keener — verify on the linked tables before paying a single-case premium.
  • Anyone who needs closed-loop inverter comms: confirm the specific 200Ah model speaks your inverter's protocol; rack modules publish compatibility lists more consistently.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a 48V 200Ah LiFePO4 battery cost?

There is exactly 1 standalone listing in the maker stores we track today: $2,151 ($0.210/Wh) for the wall-mounted unit in the table. The other route to the label — the 200Ah configuration of a shared 100/200Ah product page — was feed-priced at $2,599.99 for its 10kWh configuration in the November 21, 2026 catalog pull. Two 100Ah modules usually undercut both; that table is live on the 100Ah page.

How many kWh is a 48V 200Ah battery?

It depends on the maker's math: 48V × 200Ah = 9.6kWh, while a true 51.2V LiFePO4 pack × 200Ah = 10.24kWh. Both are sold as '48V 200Ah'. Check the watt-hour figure — the table on this page shows it for every listing.

Is one 48V 200Ah battery cheaper than two 100Ah modules?

Usually not on $/Wh — the 100Ah module market is larger and more competitive, and rack modules are routinely the cheapest stored energy in the 48V catalog. The 200Ah unit buys install simplicity instead. Both tables are live; compare them the same day.

Can I connect 48V 200Ah batteries in parallel?

Most models in this class support parallel connection to grow the bank (limits vary, commonly 4–16 units). Match brand and model, and confirm the limit and communications wiring on the product page before ordering.
