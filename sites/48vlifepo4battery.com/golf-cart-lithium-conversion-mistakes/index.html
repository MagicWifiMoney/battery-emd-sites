Mistakes 1–3: the electrical mismatches

1. Keeping the lead-acid charger. The single most common conversion failure. A 48V lead-acid cart charger is built to push a finishing voltage lead-acid likes and, on many models, to run an automatic equalization stage — voltages a LiFePO4 pack's BMS will (correctly) refuse by disconnecting, mid-charge, every charge. If the old charger has no lithium profile and cannot cap output at 58.4V with equalization fully off, it is not a charger for your new pack; it is a recurring fault. Budget the LiFePO4-profile charger as part of the conversion, not as an accessory — the full settings table is in the 48V charging guide.

2. Buying amp-hours and ignoring discharge current. A cart controller does not sip: acceleration and hills ask for short, heavy current draws, and the pack's continuous discharge rating (set by its cells and BMS) is the number that decides whether the cart pulls cleanly or sags and cuts out under load. This is where the table's small rows mislead the unwary: a single 30Ah GC2-format pack is a building block meant to be paralleled into a set, not a whole cart battery, and its current ceiling is a fraction of a 100Ah pack's. Match the pack (or paralleled set) to your controller's rating first; the $/Wh column only gets a vote after the current math passes.

3. Forgetting the cart's own charge gatekeeper. Some carts — Club Car's onboard computer is the famous case — route charging through electronics programmed around lead-acid behavior, and a lithium pack behind them charges erratically or not at all until the conversion addresses it (a bypass or a kit designed for the cart). It is also why complete conversion kits exist as a category: one row in the table below is a battery-plus-hardware kit priced as a single product. Before ordering any pack, search your exact cart model plus 'OBC' and 'lithium conversion' — ten minutes of reading beats a weekend of troubleshooting.

Mistakes 4–7: the physical and system oversights

4. Reusing tired cables and skipping the fuse. Conversion day reveals cables that were 'fine' for years: green corrosion at the lugs, insulation rubbed thin, a main cable one gauge optimistic. Lead-acid's internal resistance forgave some of that; lithium's low resistance delivers fault current far faster, which makes a proper main fuse or breaker and clean, correctly gauged cables more important after the swap, not less. If a cable looks questionable next to the old pack, it does not get promoted to the new one.

5. Being surprised by the missing 250 pounds. A six-battery 48V lead-acid set typically weighs in the region of 350–400 lb; a single 100Ah lithium pack is roughly a quarter of that (typical class figures, not tracked prices). The cart will ride higher, handle lighter and stop differently, and weight that used to sit low and central is simply gone. None of this is a reason to stay with lead — the acceleration and hill-climbing gains are the point of converting — but check the pack is properly secured in the tray (an unsecured 100 lb pack in a cart that now corners lighter is its own mistake), and expect to re-learn the cart's braking feel on the first drives.

6. Mixing chemistries or models 'temporarily'. One old lead-acid left in the string, or a second lithium pack of a different model paralleled in later, unbalances everything: the odd unit charges and discharges on a different curve, ages fast, and drags the bank with it. A conversion is all one chemistry, one brand, one model — and if range anxiety is the worry, the honest fix is the next capacity step in the table, not a hybrid string.

7. No plan for a cold garage. Carts live in unheated sheds, and a LiFePO4 pack must not be charged below freezing (0°C / 32°F) — discharging in the cold is fine, charging plates lithium and permanently costs capacity. If your cart charges outdoors through a northern winter, low-temperature charge protection on the pack's BMS is not a premium feature, it is the feature — verify it on the product page of any row below, or make 'charge it in the warm' a hard household rule.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common golf cart lithium conversion mistake?

Keeping the lead-acid charger. Its finishing and equalization voltages exceed what a 51.2V LiFePO4 pack accepts (58.4V cap), so the pack's BMS disconnects mid-charge, repeatedly. A LiFePO4-profile 58.4V charger — or an old charger verifiably set to one — is part of the conversion cost.

How big a lithium battery does a 48V golf cart need?

Most conversions land on a single 100Ah-class pack (5.12kWh) — the cheapest of them in the live table is the LiTime 48V 100Ah Bluetooth Lithium Battery | For Golf Carts and Lawn Mowers — 1 Pack 51.2V 100Ah at $1,000. Smaller GC2-format packs are building blocks to parallel into a set, not standalone cart batteries. Whatever the capacity, the pack's continuous discharge rating must cover the controller's draw; capacity alone does not make a pack suitable.

Can I mix one lithium battery with my remaining lead-acid batteries?

No. The two chemistries charge and discharge on different curves and at different voltages per unit, so a mixed string unbalances immediately: the lithium unit is overworked, the lead-acid units are undercharged, and both age fast. Convert the whole string at once.
