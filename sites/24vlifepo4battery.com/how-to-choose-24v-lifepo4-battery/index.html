Step 1 — confirm 24V is actually your voltage

24V is a means to an end: it halves the current (and cable size) of a 12V system at the same wattage, which is why it owns three niches — 24V trolling motors (70 lb thrust and up), mid-size inverters (roughly 1,500–3,000W, where 12V currents get unwieldy), and 24V vehicle systems (trucks, some RVs and military/commercial platforms). If your motor is a 12V or 36V model, or your inverter is a 48V hybrid, no 24V battery belongs in the cart — our sister trackers cover 12V and 48V instead. If you already own 12V packs, the series vs native 24V guide covers building 24V from pairs.

Step 2 — size in watt-hours, not amp-hours

Watt-hours are what your loads consume: watts × hours. A 24V trolling motor at a 45A cruise draw pulls about 1,150W — a 100Ah pack (2,560Wh) is roughly two hours at that draw, a day at mixed speeds. An RV running lights, fans, a fridge and device charging might use 1,500–2,500Wh a day, which is why the 230Ah class (5,888Wh) exists. LiFePO4 can use 80–100% of its rating (unlike lead-acid's ~50%), so size to your real daily number plus a weather margin, not to double it.

  • 50–60Ah (1,280–1,536Wh): a morning of trolling, portable power, tight battery boxes.
  • 100Ah (2,560Wh): the default — full-day trolling, weekend RV loads, small solar banks.
  • 230Ah (5,888Wh): multi-day off-grid, trucks and cabins, one-case banks.

Step 3 — the BMS rating is a hard gate, not a feature

Every LiFePO4 battery's BMS caps its continuous discharge current. Your biggest load must fit under it: a 112 lb-thrust motor at full draw (~55A) or a 2,000W inverter on 24V (~85A plus surge) will trip a 50A BMS no matter how good the battery's $/Wh is. Check the continuous rating on the product page of any row you shortlist — makers state it there — and leave surge headroom for inverter startup loads. This is the one spec where the cheapest row in the table can simply be wrong for your system.

Step 4 — cold weather and monitoring, then $/Wh

Two features separate otherwise-identical rows. Low-temperature charge protection (a BMS cutoff that refuses charging below freezing) is the minimum for any battery that will charge outdoors in winter; self-heating goes further and warms the cells so charging can proceed — the heated rows in the table name it in their titles. Bluetooth monitoring ends state-of-charge guesswork, the most common complaint about budget packs. With voltage, size, BMS and weather features settled, rank the survivors by $/Wh and buy the top row — that is exactly the order the table below is sorted in.

Frequently asked questions

What size 24V LiFePO4 battery do I need?

Work in watt-hours: add up watts × hours for everything the battery must run. The classes tracked here are 50–60Ah (1,280–1,536Wh) for a morning of trolling or portable use, 100Ah (2,560Wh) as the full-day default, and 230Ah (5,888Wh) for multi-day off-grid and truck/RV banks.

Can I use a 24V battery with a 12V trolling motor or inverter?

No. Voltage classes must match: a 24V battery needs a 24V motor, charger and inverter. Feeding 24V into 12V equipment damages it, and a 12V charger cannot charge a 24V battery.

Do I need a heated 24V battery?

Only if the battery will be charged below freezing — early-season fishing, winter RV use, unheated cabins. Low-temperature charge protection (a cutoff) is the minimum; self-heating models can actually charge in the cold. Indoors or in mild climates, skip the premium.
