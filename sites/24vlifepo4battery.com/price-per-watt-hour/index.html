Price per Watt-Hour: The One Number That Compares 24V Batteries Fairly

Batteries are sold by sticker price and amp-hours, and both mislead: a 50Ah pack and a 230Ah pack are not comparable until you price the energy itself. Price per watt-hour ($/Wh) = price ÷ watt-hours stored. Across the 19 24V models tracked here, stored energy currently runs from $0.156/Wh (Eco-Worthy) to $0.816/Wh, with a median of $0.211/Wh. This page shows how the number is computed, where it settles arguments, and where it stops being the deciding factor.

The arithmetic, on real rows

Take the two ends of today's table. The cheapest stored energy is the ECO-WORTHY 24V 100Ah Bluetooth Lithium Battery with BMS — 1 Pack at $400 — 100Ah · 2,560Wh, which is $0.156/Wh. A compact 50Ah trolling pack stores 1,280Wh; even at a lower sticker it routinely lands above $0.25/Wh, because small cases carry the same BMS, terminals and shipping as big ones spread over fewer watt-hours. The 230Ah class stores 5,888Wh in one case and prices that energy near the bottom of the table. Same chemistry, same voltage — the $/Wh column is the difference between buying a battery and buying energy.

The conversion is always the same: watt-hours = volts × amp-hours, using nominal LiFePO4 voltage (25.6V for a '24V' battery). A 100Ah pack is 2,560Wh whether the listing shouts the Ah or hides it. $/kWh, used on our sister trackers for home storage, is the identical number × 1,000 — $0.156/Wh is the same fact as the cheapest row's $/kWh column.

Where $/Wh settles the argument — and where it doesn't

  • Same size, different brands: $/Wh is a pure price fight. Two 100Ah packs store identical energy; the cheaper $/Wh is simply the cheaper battery.
  • Different sizes: $/Wh tells you what the extra capacity costs. If a 230Ah pack prices energy 20% below a 100Ah pack, you are paying a 20% premium for the smaller case's convenience — sometimes worth it, now at least visible.
  • Bundles: multi-packs appear in the table at their bundle price with the bundle's total energy. A 2-pack whose $/Wh beats the single unit is a real discount; one that doesn't is packaging.

What $/Wh does not price: the BMS continuous discharge rating (a trolling motor or inverter cares deeply), self-heating and low-temperature protection, Bluetooth monitoring, case format (a Group 31 drop-in vs a plain box), and warranty length. The method on this site is deliberate: rank by $/Wh so the price of energy is never hidden, then read the row's own product page for the features that justify a premium. A battery 15% above the median $/Wh with the heating and monitoring you need is a better buy than the table-topper without them.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate price per watt-hour?

Divide the price by the battery's watt-hours. Watt-hours = nominal volts × amp-hours: a 24V (25.6V nominal) 100Ah battery stores 2,560Wh, so a $520 price is $520 ÷ 2,560 = $0.203/Wh. Every row on this site has the math already done.

What is a good $/Wh for a 24V LiFePO4 battery?

It moves with maker pricing, so this page quotes the live range instead of a fixed benchmark: currently $0.156/Wh at the low end and a $0.211/Wh median across 19 tracked models. In general the 100Ah and 230Ah classes price energy lowest; compact 50–60Ah packs cost more per watt-hour.

Is $/Wh the same as $/kWh?

Yes — $/kWh is $/Wh × 1,000. Home-storage shoppers usually see $/kWh (a 5.12kWh module at $0.170/Wh is $170/kWh); smaller batteries are easier to compare in $/Wh. Both columns are shown in every table here.
