12V LiFePO4 in Series and Parallel: Volts Up or Amp-Hours Up

Most 12V LiFePO4 batteries can be wired in series, in parallel, or both — and the two moves do opposite jobs. Series adds voltage: two packs in series are a 24V battery, four are a 48V battery, and the amp-hours do not change. Parallel adds amp-hours at the same 12V. The catch is that the permission slip lives in each model's spec sheet, not in the chemistry: many models cap at 4 in series, some budget packs are parallel-only, and every bank must be one brand, one model, one age. The limits: 4 in series is common, guaranteed is not

There is no chemistry-wide wiring limit — there is a per-model one, set by the BMS. Series is the demanding direction: in a 4S string each battery's BMS sits at a different point in a 51.2V circuit, and the pack must be built and rated for it. That is why the published ceilings cluster where they do — up to 4 in series is the common rating among models that allow series at all, parallel allowances are usually equal or larger, and a meaningful minority of budget packs are parallel-only. A model whose spec sheet is silent on series has answered the question: do not series-wire it.

  • Same brand, model, capacity and age — all four, in both directions. The bank behaves like its weakest unit, and mixed units make that unit weaker.
  • Buy the bank at once where you can. A second purchase a year later is a different production batch; if you must expand, do it early and re-check voltages before connecting (next section).
  • The charger follows the string, not the battery. A 2S bank charges at ~29.2V and a 4S bank at 58.4V — your 12V charger cannot charge a series string as a unit, and the inverter/charger must be a model for the target voltage.
  • Discharge current does not multiply in series. A 4S string of 100A-BMS batteries is still a 100A battery system, just at 51.2V. Parallel is the direction that adds current.

Before ordering for a wired bank, open the product page for your exact model and find its series/parallel line — the 100Ah table and the 200Ah table link every tracked model to its maker page, which is where that line lives.

Parallel wiring that shares current fairly

  1. Charge every battery individually to full with a 12V LiFePO4 charger (14.2–14.6V — the charging guide has the profile), let them rest, and confirm the resting voltages sit within about a tenth of a volt of each other. Paralleling a full pack with an empty one sends a large equalizing current through the interconnects the moment the terminals meet.
  2. Connect negatives, then positives, with the bank disconnected from loads and charger.
  3. Take power off diagonally: the main positive lead on the first battery, the main negative lead on the last. Diagonal take-off forces current through every interconnect instead of letting the nearest battery do all the work. On banks of three or more, a busbar with equal-length leads per battery does the same job better.
  4. Fuse the bank — a fuse on the main positive sized to your cabling and loads, as close to the batteries as practical.

Done this way, a parallel bank behaves like one big battery: one charger, one state of charge, current shared roughly evenly. Done carelessly — mismatched voltages, corner take-off, mixed models — the nearest or strongest pack cycles hardest, ages fastest, and quietly caps the whole bank. The same fairness logic is why the solar sizing guide treats the bank, not the individual battery, as the unit the panels must fill.

Series strings: the two ways they fail

Failure one — the string goes dark. In series there is one current path, so if any single battery's BMS disconnects (over-current, a temperature limit, a fault), the whole string stops — a 48V inverter fed by four 12V packs has no partial mode. Size the string so one pack's discharge rating comfortably covers the load, and treat a string shutdown as a per-battery diagnosis, not a system failure.

Failure two — drift. Each BMS balances the cells inside its own battery, but nothing balances one battery against the next. Over months the packs settle at slightly different states of charge; the weakest reaches empty first and caps the string's usable capacity, and reaches full first on charge and trips early. The maintenance is cheap and scheduled: every few months, or whenever runtime sags, charge each battery individually to full and re-check the set — the same individual charge you did before assembly. An external battery equalizer wired across the string can slow drift, but it does not replace matched batteries or the periodic individual charge.

Frequently asked questions

How many 12V LiFePO4 batteries can I connect in series?

Four is the common published ceiling — 4S gives 51.2V, a 48V system — for models that allow series at all. It is a per-model specification, not a chemistry rule: some models allow fewer, and some budget packs prohibit series entirely. The product page for your exact model is the authority; never exceed its stated limit.

Can I wire a 100Ah and a 200Ah LiFePO4 battery in parallel?

Makers advise against mixing capacities, brands, models or ages in one bank. In parallel, different internal resistances share current unevenly and the packs sit at different depths of discharge; in series, a mixed string is capped by its smallest battery and drifts out of balance quickly. Build the bank from identical units bought together — matched sets are also the honest way to use the $/Wh table on the homepage, since the price is per matched battery.

Do batteries in a series string need a balancer?

Each battery's internal BMS balances its own cells, but nothing balances one whole battery against the next in a string. Standard practice: charge every battery individually to full before assembly, then re-check individual voltages every few months and top up any that drift. An external equalizer across the string can slow the drift, but it is an addition to that routine, not a substitute for it.
