Van Life Battery Sizing: Audit the Day Before You Buy the Battery

Most van batteries are the wrong size for the same reason: they were bought from a forum post instead of a load audit. The audit takes ten minutes — list what runs, for how long, and add the watt-hours. A frugal weekend van audits near 700Wh a day; a full-time van with a fridge, heater and remote-work gear audits near 1,500–2,000Wh a day — and those two vans need different battery classes. The van-sized class tracked here (100Ah to 314Ah, priced in the live table below) runs from $0.112/Wh; this worksheet lands you on the right row of it.

Planning ranges, not measurements — your fridge's duty cycle in a hot van and your heater's in a cold one move their rows the most, which is why winter full-timers audit a second, colder version of this table. Two rows deserve emphasis: the fridge is the only load that never sleeps, and the satellite internet row rivals it — a remote-work van's battery is mostly a fridge-and-Starlink battery. The fridge row's own math, by fridge class and ambient temperature, is worked in the fridge runtime guide. Inverter-powered loads (an induction hob, a kettle) draw hard but briefly; count them at their real minutes, not their sticker watts × a guessed hour.

Step 2 — the formula, and the class each total lands in

Amp-hours to buy = daily Wh × days between charges ÷ (12.8V × 0.8). The 0.8 is deliberate headroom: plan to use about 80% of nameplate so a cloudy day or a long drive-less stretch is an inconvenience, not a dead fridge. Worked at two days between charges:

Note where the honest answers land: the single 100Ah pack (1,280Wh) is a one-day battery for all but the leanest audits, which is why the table below starts at the 100Ah class but the worksheet keeps walking past it. The $/Wh pattern helps the bigger answer: in the live table the 314Ah class holds the catalog floor ($0.112/Wh — the ECO-WORTHY EnergyTrek 12V 314Ah LiFePO4 Lithium Battery with SOC LEDs and Low-temp Protection at $449), because one case and one BMS carry 4,019Wh. In vans, unusually, the bigger battery is both the capacity answer and the value answer — if it fits the build's battery box. The feature checks behind whichever class you land on — low-temperature protection, charge rate, monitoring — are the buying guide's list.

Step 3 — size the refill, not just the tank

A van battery is refilled three ways, and the weakest of them sets your real autonomy. Driving: a DC-DC charger fed by the alternator typically restores a few hundred watt-hours per driving hour — a van that moves every second day can run a class smaller than the formula's answer. Roof solar: a van roof fits roughly 400–800W of panels, which in summer replaces a frugal day's audit and in winter replaces a fraction of it; the 12V solar page prices that pairing. Shore power: resets everything, and vans that camp plugged-in half the time should not buy batteries for the other half's worst case. Overnight medical loads belong in the audit too: a CPAP's nights per charge, by humidifier setting, are worked in the CPAP battery guide.

  • Stationary in winter is the sizing case: no driving, short solar days, heater running — if the battery survives that audit, every other season is easy.
  • Charge speed is a spec too: check the pack's maximum charge current against your DC-DC charger's output; a big bank behind a small charger is a slow bank.
  • Stay at 12V in a van: alternator charging and native 12V loads are the vehicle's whole electrical ecosystem — the case for ever going 48V in a stationary build is laid out on our sister site's 48V vs 12V comparison, and a van is not it.

Frequently asked questions

How big a battery do I need for van life?

From your audit: amp-hours = daily watt-hours × days between charges ÷ (12.8 × 0.8). A ~700Wh/day weekend van at two days lands near 137Ah (buy the 200Ah class); a ~1,200Wh/day full-time van lands near 234Ah (the 314Ah class); a winter remote-work van near 2,000Wh/day needs roughly 391Ah — a 314Ah plus a paralleled second pack.

Is 100Ah enough for a van?

For a lean one-day-at-a-time setup, yes — 1,280Wh nameplate, about 1,024Wh planned-usable, covers a fridge, lights and phones for roughly a day, and daily driving or solar keeps it topped up. It is not enough for two stationary days with a fridge in summer heat, which is the trip profile that strands most 100Ah vans.

Why does the 314Ah class cost less per watt-hour?

Fixed costs — case, BMS, terminals — spread over 4,019Wh instead of 1,280Wh, and 314Ah is the industry's high-volume cell format. The live table shows the result: the class currently holds this catalog's $/Wh floor. The trade-offs are weight (a ~60–70 lb class lift) and a single point of failure versus two smaller paralleled packs.
