Charging a 12V LiFePO4 Battery: The 14.4V Profile

Charge a 12V LiFePO4 at 14.2–14.6V absorption (14.4V is the safe middle), with no equalization and no required float — that is the whole profile, and most charging damage comes from chargers that can't do it. Below: the settings, how long charging actually takes by charger size, and the cold-weather rule that matters more than both.

Resting voltage is not charging voltage. The voltages in both tables are charging voltages — what a charger applies while current is flowing. A resting voltage is measured with the charger and loads disconnected, after the pack has sat long enough for its voltage to settle: a fully charged 12V LiFePO4 rests at about 13.3–13.4V, and it will never rest at 14.4V. Reading 13.3V on a disconnected, rested pack does not mean it needs more charge, and seeing 14.4V on the terminals while a charger is connected tells you the charger's setting, not the state of charge. Judge charge state from a rested reading (or a shunt/Bluetooth readout), and judge the charger from the charging tables.

LiFePO4's flat voltage curve is why the times are nearly linear: unlike lead-acid, it accepts full current until ~90% charged, so a 20A charger really does halve the 10A time. It's also why voltage alone can't tell you the state of charge — a resting 13.3V and 13.1V can be 60% and 25%. Use a shunt monitor or a Bluetooth BMS readout, not a voltmeter guess. These times are per battery: a bank wired in series charges at the string's voltage, not 14.4V — the series-parallel guide covers bank wiring and string charging.

The cold rule, and the charger you already own

Never charge below freezing without protection. Charging a frozen LiFePO4 plates lithium metal onto the cells — permanent capacity loss, invisible until the battery fades early. If the battery will live anywhere that gets cold (RV, boat shed, ice season), low-temperature charge protection in the BMS is the feature that matters most; it is called out per-model in our 100Ah comparison. The ice fishing guide works the same rule from the other side — discharge cold, charge warm.

Your existing lead-acid charger is fine if it has a lithium mode or its voltages land in the table above with equalization off. Many RV converters have a lithium switch on newer models; older ones charge to ~13.6–14.4V without equalizing and will fill a LiFePO4 to roughly 90% — usable, not ideal. When in doubt, the battery maker's listed profile wins over any generic advice, including this page.

Frequently asked questions

What voltage fully charges a 12V LiFePO4 battery?

14.2–14.6V at the charger (absorption), after which the pack rests at about 13.3–13.4V fully charged. The pack is 12.8V nominal — '12V' is the class name, not the charged voltage.

Can I leave a LiFePO4 battery on a float charger?

A lithium-aware float at ~13.5V is fine for standby duty. What's harmful is a lead-acid maintainer that periodically pushes high-voltage desulfation pulses. For storage, the healthiest state is ~50–80% charge, charger disconnected.

Why does my LiFePO4 battery stop charging in winter?

If it has low-temperature protection, the BMS is doing its job: it blocks charging below 0°C to prevent lithium plating. Warm the battery (use, sun, a heated space) and charging resumes. Models without the protection will accept the charge and quietly lose capacity.

What size charger do I need for a 12V 100Ah LiFePO4 battery?

A 20A lithium-profile charger is the common sweet spot: it refills a 100Ah pack from empty in about 5 hours. A 10A charger takes roughly 10 hours (fine overnight), and 50A — the maximum charge rate most makers allow for a 100Ah pack (0.5C) — takes about 2 hours. Whatever the size, the charger must hold a 14.2–14.6V absorption profile with equalization off.
