LiFePO4 vs Lead-Acid: The Sticker Lies, the Cycle Count Doesn't

Compare stickers and lead-acid wins: a 12V 100Ah AGM battery typically retails around $180–$250 (a market range — we don't track lead-acid prices), while the cheapest tracked 100Ah LiFePO4 in the table below is the ⚡Flash Sale⚡LiTime Starry Sea LiFePO4 Battery — 12.8V 100Ah Group24 Bluetooth Battery at $190. The lead-acid figures are standard planning numbers for the chemistry (50% depth-of-discharge, ~500 cycles at that depth, ~$200 typical retail) — labeled as such because this site tracks LiFePO4 prices live and lead-acid prices not at all. The LiFePO4 cycle figure is deliberately conservative: makers in this class commonly rate 4,000+ cycles, and we used 3,000 so the comparison does not depend on believing a datasheet. Even if you halve LiFePO4's life and double lead-acid's, lithium still wins the per-kWh ledger several times over. The gap is structural — usable fraction × cycle count — not a sale price.

What ten years of daily cycling actually costs

Run the worked example as a calendar. A daily-cycled lead-acid bank reaches its ~500 cycles in under a year and a half, so a ten-year RV, cabin or solar installation buys the same battery six or seven times — roughly $1,200–$1,400 of lead-acid purchases, plus the labor of swapping 60 lb batteries and the capacity fade in between (a lead-acid at cycle 400 is not the battery you bought). The LiFePO4 side of the ledger is one purchase at the live prices below, with a second battery a plausible year-ten event rather than a scheduled one.

  • Weight is a cost too: ~60 lb vs ~24 lb for the same 100Ah slot — in a van or boat that difference is fuel, payload and handling, every trip.
  • Charge efficiency: lead-acid returns roughly 80–85% of what you put in (and crawls through its last 20%); LiFePO4 returns ~95%+ and accepts full current almost to full. On solar, the wasted fifth of a lead-acid charge is panels you bought for nothing.
  • The honest exception: a battery that barely cycles — an engine starter, or a backup pack floated for years and used twice — never lives long enough to collect LiFePO4's cycle dividend. There, lead-acid's low sticker is a real saving, not a trap. LiFePO4 also cannot crank an engine unless the model is specifically rated for it.

So the decision rule is a single question: how many cycles will this battery actually serve? Daily or weekly cycling (solar, RV living, trolling, off-grid) makes LiFePO4 the cheaper battery by a wide margin. Rare-use standby keeps lead-acid rational. Everything else — brand, Bluetooth, case color — is priced in the table.

Making the swap without buying twice

  • Charger profile: your existing converter works only if it can hold a lithium profile (14.2–14.6V, no equalization) — the charging guide has the settings table.
  • Size by usable Wh, not Ah: one 100Ah LiFePO4 replaces roughly two 100Ah lead-acids in delivered energy, so a like-for-like Ah swap over-buys capacity — a pleasant surprise, but budget for the bank you need.
  • Low-temperature protection matters if the battery charges anywhere unheated; lead-acid tolerates cold charging that damages unprotected lithium.

Frequently asked questions

Is LiFePO4 worth the extra cost over lead-acid?

For anything cycled regularly, yes — on planning figures, a 100Ah LiFePO4 delivers roughly ten times the lifetime energy of a 100Ah AGM at a similar sticker, putting its cost per delivered kWh near $0.06 against lead-acid's ~$0.63. For a rarely cycled starter or standby battery, lead-acid's cheaper sticker can be the rational buy.

How many lead-acid batteries equal one LiFePO4?

In delivered energy over a working life, roughly six to seven 100Ah AGMs match one 100Ah LiFePO4 (~500 usable cycles each against ~3,000+). In per-cycle usable capacity alone, one LiFePO4 roughly equals two lead-acids of the same Ah rating, because it can use ~80% of its capacity against lead-acid's ~50%.

Can I just drop a LiFePO4 battery into my lead-acid system?

Mechanically yes — same voltage class, same terminals. Electrically, check two things first: the charger needs a lithium profile (14.2–14.6V absorption, no equalization or desulfation), and alternator-charged systems (vans, boats) need a DC-DC charger so the alternator is not asked to feed a low-resistance lithium bank directly.
