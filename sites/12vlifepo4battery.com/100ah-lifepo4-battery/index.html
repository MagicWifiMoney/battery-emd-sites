Amp-hours measure charge, not energy. Comparing a 12V 100Ah at $200 against a 48V 100Ah at $900 by sticker — or by 'cost per Ah' — gets the answer backwards: the 48V battery holds 4× the energy. The only fair cross-voltage unit is $/Wh, which is how every table here is sorted.

Buying the 12V version: the three checks that matter

  • Group size: Group 24 and Group 31 are the common cases; measure your battery box before ordering — a 100Ah label says nothing about dimensions.
  • Low-temperature protection: a BMS that blocks charging below freezing is essential for RV, marine and ice-season use; not every budget 100Ah has it.
  • Series rating: if you might wire two for 24V later, the model must be series-rated (many are 4S-capable; some budget packs are parallel-only).

Beyond those, the table is the decision: every row stores the same 1,280Wh, so the $/Wh column is a pure price ranking. The dedicated 12V 100Ah page goes deeper on this class; the buying guide covers the rest of the checklist.

Frequently asked questions

How many watt-hours is a 100Ah LiFePO4 battery?

It depends on voltage: 12V × 100Ah = 1,280Wh (at 12.8V nominal), 24V = 2,560Wh, 48V = 5,120Wh. Always convert to watt-hours before comparing prices across voltages.

How long will a 12V 100Ah LiFePO4 battery run a fridge?

A modern fridge draws roughly 1–1.5kWh per day. A 12V 100Ah battery holds 1,280Wh, of which about 1,090Wh is usable through an inverter — so expect most of a day to a full day for the fridge alone, longer for efficient 12V compressor fridges that skip the inverter.

Is a 100Ah LiFePO4 better than two 50Ah?

Usually, on price: one 100Ah pack has one BMS and one case, so its $/Wh is typically lower, and wiring is simpler. Two smaller packs give redundancy and fit split battery boxes. The table's $/Wh column settles the price side for the models tracked here.
